Photophosphorylation is the process by which light energy is converted into chemical energy during photosynthesis. It occurs in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts and is essential for the production of ATP, the energy currency of cells.

There are two main types of photophosphorylation: cyclic and non-cyclic.

Non-cyclic photophosphorylation (also called the Z-scheme) involves both Photosystem II and Photosystem I. When light is absorbed by chlorophyll in PSII, water is split to release electrons, protons, and oxygen. The electrons travel through an electron transport chain, releasing energy that pumps protons across the thylakoid membrane. The resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthase to produce ATP. The electrons eventually reach PSI, where they are re-energized by light and used to reduce NADP+ to NADPH.

Cyclic photophosphorylation involves only Photosystem I. When light energizes electrons in PSI, they can return to the electron transport chain between PSI and the cytochrome complex, cycling back to PSI. This process generates additional ATP without producing NADPH or oxygen. Cyclic phosphorylation is thought to be an adaptation that allows the cell to balance ATP and NADPH production based on the Calvin cycle's needs.

The chemiosmotic hypothesis, proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961, explains how the proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane drives ATP synthesis. ATP synthase, a large enzyme complex, allows protons to flow back into the stroma, and this flow is coupled to the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP.

Key terms to remember:
- Thylakoid: membrane-bound compartment inside chloroplasts where light-dependent reactions occur
- Stroma: fluid-filled space surrounding the thylakoids
- Granum: stack of thylakoids
- Calvin cycle: light-independent reactions that use ATP and NADPH to fix CO2 into glucose
- Photolysis: splitting of water to release electrons, protons, and oxygen
- P680 and P700: reaction center chlorophylls of PSII and PSI respectively
